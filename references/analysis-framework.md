# Analysis Framework

Use the lenses that materially explain the supplied text. Do not force empty sections or turn every observation into a score.

## Core lenses

- **Overview and reader promise:** premise, genre signals, central dramatic question, opening contract, expected reading experience, and where the first meaningful payoff occurs.
- **Plot and structure:** main and secondary lines, causes and consequences, conflicts, stakes, turning points, escalation, climax, resolution, parallel structure, and chapter or volume architecture.
- **Characters:** names and aliases, narrative role, desires, fears, tactics, traits, internal contradictions, irreversible choices, changes, and important appearances.
- **Relationships:** participants, direction, type, mutuality, power balance, phases, obstacles, changes over time, final state, and what each side appears to need from the bond.
- **World and setting:** locations, atmosphere, social order, institutions, culture, politics, economy, geography, technology or power systems, rules, costs, limits, and consistency.
- **Themes and ideas:** propositions the story appears to argue, evidence for them, meaningful counterarguments, and whether the ending confirms, complicates, or rejects them. State a claim such as “mercy requires power,” not a topic word such as “mercy.”
- **Symbols and motifs:** recurring objects, colors, images, phrases, actions, or settings; track their context, evolution, contrasts, and possible meanings. Repetition alone does not prove symbolism.
- **Information and suspense:** what the reader and major characters know, information gaps, withholding, misdirection, dramatic irony, clues, reveals, reversals, and how later facts reframe earlier scenes.
- **Foreshadowing:** setup, expected or alternative payoff, actual payoff when present, and evidence for both ends. A motif can create atmosphere without predicting an event.
- **Timeline:** narrated order versus story order, explicit and relative time, duration, flashbacks, flash-forwards, parallel events, gaps, and contradictions.
- **Scene and chapter craft:** each important unit's entry pressure, goal, conflict, concrete action, turn, consequence, exit tension, and function in the larger story. Summarize ordinary connective units instead of mechanically analyzing every scene.
- **Perspective and voice:** narrator, focalizer, person and tense, narrative distance, viewpoint shifts, access to interiority, direct thought versus free indirect discourse when relevant, narrator reliability, and character-voice distinction.
- **Prose style:** use the integrated prose-and-style lens below. In a broad report, summarize the traits that most shape the reading experience; in a style-focused request, examine all supported dimensions.
- **Reader experience:** curiosity, expectation, tension, surprise, emotional movement, payoff, release, aftereffect, and chapter-end propulsion. Describe effects supported by the text; do not claim that every reader will react identically.
- **Uncertainty:** missing text, unreliable narration, disputed aliases, ambiguous motives, conflicting rules or chronology, multiple valid readings, and unseen outcomes.

## Prose and style lens

Distinguish **prose craft**, which describes how particular passages work, from **authorial style**, which requires stable patterns across separated samples. One excerpt supports only a local passage profile. For a whole-work style claim, sample the opening, middle, ending, dialogue-heavy, action-heavy, reflective, and descriptive passages when available, then report meaningful drift between them.

- **Sentence design:** typical length and variation, simple versus layered syntax, coordination and subordination, fragments, ellipsis, inversion, parallel construction, sentence openers, and active or passive emphasis.
- **Paragraph design:** paragraph length and density, one-action versus multi-action units, topic progression, deliberate line breaks, whitespace, and how paragraphs accelerate, pause, or redirect attention.
- **Diction and register:** concrete versus abstract wording, colloquial versus formal or classical register, verb strength, modifier density, jargon or idiom, recurring lexical fields, repetition, and shifts between narration and dialogue.
- **Rhythm and cadence:** long-short alternation, punctuation-driven pauses, accumulation and release, list rhythm, repetition, sound pattern when perceptible, and whether cadence changes with tension or emotion.
- **Narrative voice:** narrator attitude, warmth, distance, authority, irony, restraint, reliability, and how the voice blends with or separates from the focal character. Coordinate this with the perspective lens rather than repeating it.
- **Interiority and emotion:** direct labels, direct thought, free indirect discourse, behavioral cues, bodily sensation, environmental reflection, silence, and ambiguity; explain what each choice lets the reader infer.
- **Dialogue craft:** subtext, turn-taking, interruption, evasion, silence, speech rhythm, vocabulary and syntax by character, dialogue tags, action beats, exposition load, and whether voices remain distinguishable without name tags.
- **Description and sensory selection:** which senses dominate, static versus dynamic description, concrete detail, spatial clarity, selective omission, atmosphere, and whether description also advances character, conflict, theme, or information.
- **Imagery and rhetoric:** metaphor, simile, personification, metonymy, contrast, repetition, parallelism, irony, allusion, rhetorical questions, and image networks. Explain function and effect instead of merely naming devices.
- **Transitions and continuity:** movement between sentences, paragraphs, scenes, times, locations, viewpoints, and chapters; note hard cuts, bridges, callbacks, and whether transitions create clarity, surprise, or productive disorientation.
- **Tone and adaptation:** dominant tone, tonal range, control of comedy and gravity, changes across characters or scene types, and whether variation is purposeful or inconsistent.
- **Signature patterns:** features that recur across multiple separated samples and distinguish the work from generic prose. Separate stable traits from one-off flourishes, genre conventions, translation artifacts, and formatting imposed by the source.
- **Strengths and limitations:** identify what a technique achieves and where repetition, vagueness, excess, monotony, ornament, exposition, or mismatch weakens it. Do not treat ornate prose as automatically good or plain prose as automatically weak.

Use counts or percentages only when they were actually calculated from a defined sample. Otherwise use directional descriptions such as “mostly short declarative sentences” and state the sample boundary. Support major style findings with short, representative passages and locators; do not reconstruct or imitate the source.

## Conditional lenses

- **Commercial web fiction:** reader need, repeatable story engine, progression loop, hook and payoff cycle, emotional deficit and release, status changes, volume rhythm, and continued-reading mechanism.
- **Mystery or suspense:** central and secondary questions, evidence chain, suspect access, clue fairness, red herrings, reveal timing, solution validity, and unresolved evidence.
- **Romance or relationship-led fiction:** attraction basis, relationship phases, reciprocal evidence, obstacle type, intimacy and trust changes, power shifts, rupture, repair, and whether the ending earns its state.
- **Speculative fiction:** rule clarity, limitations and costs, interaction between world rules and plot choices, institutions, factions, geography, history, and continuity failures.
- **Literary or experimental fiction:** motif networks, ambiguity, unreliable narration, focalization, free indirect discourse, structural echoes, gaps, form-content interaction, and competing thematic readings.

## Evidence status

Call something a **fact** when the supplied text states or unambiguously shows it. Use **inference** for a supported interpretation and **uncertain** when evidence is incomplete, contradictory, or outside the supplied scope. Score or rank the work only when the user asks and the criteria are stated.
